# Ranching for Profit in Botswana: Reframing Cattle Production Around Rangeland Economics, Reproductive Performance and Climate Resilience

**Article type:** Evidence-informed perspective and applied research synthesis  
**Geographic focus:** Botswana, with explicitly identified Southern African comparisons  
**Keywords:** Ranching for Profit; Botswana cattle; ranch economics; rangeland management; Tswana cattle; drought adaptation; beef value chain; climate-smart livestock; AgTech

**Listening companion:** [Jim Gerrish — What Really Drives Profitability in Grass Farming? (Spotify)](https://open.spotify.com/episode/2jGK1JXBBEmXKWbiXOTDBk). The episode is a practitioner resource and should be treated as a prompt for local testing, not peer-reviewed Botswana evidence.

## Abstract

A large cattle herd is often interpreted as a sign of agricultural wealth, yet animal numbers alone reveal little about the economic performance or ecological sustainability of a livestock enterprise. In Botswana, where cattle production is deeply connected to rural livelihoods, national identity, export markets and semi-arid rangelands, the distinction between livestock ownership and profitable ranch management is particularly important. The challenge is not simply to increase the national herd, but to improve the quantity and value of saleable livestock output produced from each breeding female, hectare, unit of labour and pula invested, while maintaining the natural resource base.

This perspective synthesises peer-reviewed Botswana research on cattle productivity, indigenous and crossbred cow performance, forage quality, rangeland condition, rainfall variability, communal pastoralism, livestock losses, soil microbial diversity and beef value-chain economics. It places these findings alongside Southern African and international literature on grazing-system comparisons, adaptive management and drought resilience. The evidence does not support a universal claim that rotational grazing, fencing, higher stocking density or a particular breed automatically produces superior outcomes. Instead, it indicates that results depend on rainfall, ecological zone, forage composition, grazing pressure, livestock class, management capacity, market access and the quality of farm-level records.

A Botswana-adapted Ranching for Profit framework is proposed, organised around five management dimensions: financial performance, forage and rangeland productivity, herd and reproductive performance, people and operating systems, and market and climate resilience. The article also identifies practical opportunities for offline-first farm records, ranch performance assessments, forage monitoring, drought planning, livestock-loss reduction and locally grounded microbial research. The central proposition is that profitable livestock production should be evaluated through transparent biological, ecological and financial measures rather than herd size or fashionable management labels. The framework is a research and implementation agenda, not a claim of validated profit gains.

## Introduction

In Botswana, cattle can be savings, productive capital, a source of food, a livelihood asset and a symbol of social standing. This explains why herd numbers carry such cultural and economic weight. Yet the number of cattle on a farm does not, by itself, establish whether the enterprise is profitable. A cow that fails to produce a surviving calf, a bull with poor fertility, or an animal retained through several dry seasons without an adequate production rationale can consume scarce forage and working capital while contributing little to saleable output.

The more useful question is not simply how many cattle a farmer owns. It is how effectively the enterprise converts land, forage, water, labour, genetics and capital into saleable livestock while protecting the ecological system that makes production possible.

This distinction is central to the Ranching for Profit approach and to the grass-farming discussion associated with Jim Gerrish. The supplied listening resource, *What Really Drives Profitability in Grass Farming?*, invites producers to examine grass production and utilisation as economic drivers rather than treating forage as an unlimited free input (Gerrish, n.d.). The practitioner framework is valuable as a management lens, but it should not be confused with peer-reviewed evidence of outcomes in Botswana. Local application requires field validation.

Botswana is an especially important setting for this discussion. Its livestock enterprises operate across distinct ecological settings, including the Sandveld, Wet-Sandveld and Hardveld, with large differences in rainfall, soils, vegetation, land use and production opportunities. The semi-arid Kalahari has erratic rainfall and seasonal forage constraints. In the east, mixed crop-livestock systems and relatively higher rainfall occur in different soil and vegetation settings. Northern districts add complex wildlife-livestock interfaces and veterinary considerations.

National livestock ambitions therefore need to be matched by improvements in animal productivity, rangeland management, disease control, market access and farm business management. Expanding cattle numbers without a corresponding understanding of carrying capacity, forage availability and market demand could intensify pressure on land and water.

The purpose of this article is to develop a practical, evidence-informed framework for Botswana and comparable Southern African dryland systems. It asks four connected questions: What does the Botswana literature tell us about the drivers of cattle enterprise performance? What management claims are not adequately supported? How can ranchers measure financial, biological and ecological outcomes? And what low-capital services and AgTech tools could help producers make better decisions?

## Methods

This article is an applied narrative synthesis, not a registered systematic review or a new field experiment. Priority was given to peer-reviewed research directly concerning Botswana, including studies on cattle productivity, reproductive performance, grazing and vegetation, forage quality, drought, communal pastoralism, soil microbial communities, animal disease and beef value chains. Regional Southern African literature was included where it addressed comparable semi-arid rangeland processes or provided a useful methodological debate.

The evidence was interpreted according to its design and scope. Longitudinal production trials, landscape studies, observational case studies, system-dynamics models and literature reviews answer different questions and should not be treated as equivalent. Historical data were not presented as current national averages. Correlations were not interpreted as proof of causation. Regional recommendations were not automatically transferred to Botswana's ecological zones.

The analysis then translated the literature into a proposed ranch-performance framework. The proposed indicators and data fields are management recommendations requiring validation with farmers, range scientists, animal-production specialists, veterinary authorities and buyers. No illustrative financial values are represented as measured Botswana farm results.

## Results

### 1. Herd size is an incomplete measure of business performance

A cattle enterprise should distinguish between asset value, production output, cash flow and profitability. A large herd may hold considerable asset value, but it may also carry high maintenance costs, low reproductive performance or exposure to drought. Conversely, a smaller herd may achieve stronger output per breeding female or per hectare. Neither conclusion should be assumed without records.

The most useful starting indicators include the number of breeding females exposed, calves born alive, calves weaned, weaning weights where measured, mortality by cause, sales by class, feed costs, labour costs and annual operating expenses. From these, farmers can calculate calving rate, calf survival, weaning rate, kilograms of weaner produced per exposed female and gross margin by enterprise.

Definitions matter. Calving rate should be calculated as cows calving divided by breeding females exposed over a defined breeding period. Calf survival to weaning should be calculated as calves weaned divided by calves born alive in the relevant cohort. Weaning rate should be calves weaned divided by breeding females exposed. A percentage without its numerator, denominator and period can mislead.

Rennie et al. (1977) compared traditional cattle-post and fenced-ranch systems in Botswana over four years. The study reported higher calving percentage, calf weight and estimated calf output per cow in the fenced-ranch group. The results are historically important, but they do not prove that fencing alone caused the difference. Management, veterinary access, selection, forage, labour and other conditions may have differed. The study should be read as evidence that production systems and management conditions can influence output, not as a universal prescription to fence communal grazing land.

### 2. Reproductive performance is a major economic lever

The breeding herd is the engine of a cow-calf enterprise. The cost of maintaining a breeding female is incurred whether or not she produces a calf. Reproductive failure therefore affects both current output and the efficiency with which forage and capital are used.

A useful breeding programme tracks fertility, calving interval, calf survival, maternal performance, bull fertility, replacement rates and lifetime productivity. It should also record why cows are culled or retained. A cow that produces a smaller calf consistently but survives and reproduces under local conditions may have a different economic value from a larger animal with higher maintenance requirements or inconsistent fertility.

Light, Buck and Lethola (1982) examined reproductive performance, mothering ability and productivity in Tswana and crossbred beef cows in Botswana using seven years of historical records from 1974 to 1980. The reported calving rates differed among breed groups, as did calf survival. The paper demonstrates the importance of evaluating reproductive and maternal outcomes alongside growth. However, those observations are not a contemporary breed ranking. Present-day decisions require current data on lifetime kilograms weaned per cow exposed, cow maintenance, longevity, health, adaptation and buyer specifications.

The practical implication is not that indigenous cattle should always be preferred or that crossbreeding should be rejected. It is that genetic decisions should be matched to the production environment. A genotype that performs well under intensive feeding may not be the most profitable under extensive grazing, limited water and variable forage. Equally, an adapted cow that produces too little saleable output may not meet a particular commercial objective. The decision must be based on whole-system performance.

### 3. Grass is a productive asset, but biomass is not the same as nutrition

The grass-farming concept is particularly relevant to Botswana because natural grazing supplies much of the feed base for cattle. Yet forage availability is not a single variable. The amount of standing biomass, the proportion accessible to livestock, species composition, plant maturity, digestibility and nutrient content all influence animal performance.

A Kalahari study published in the *Journal of Arid Environments* in 1986 examined eight dominant grasses over three years and reported substantial species differences and seasonal variation in crude protein, phosphorus, calcium, moisture and digestibility. The measured nutrient levels were generally low relative to cattle requirements, apart from calcium, and woody species often had higher nutrient concentrations than grasses (Skarpe & Bergström, 1986).

This finding matters because a paddock that appears green or carries visible standing grass may not provide adequate nutrition for every livestock class. Lactating cows, growing calves and animals in maintenance condition have different nutritional needs. A grazing plan should therefore consider forage quality as well as quantity, especially during the dry season.

For a practical ranch assessment, field observations should record the season, dominant species or functional groups where the observer is competent, ground cover, bare ground, litter, woody encroachment and evidence of erosion. Biomass estimates should use a documented method. Where supplementation or feed procurement decisions depend on nutrient levels, representative samples should be analysed by a competent laboratory. A photograph or satellite vegetation index cannot, by itself, establish crude protein, phosphorus status or carrying capacity.

### 4. Grazing management is not a contest between labels

The debate between continuous and rotational grazing is often presented as if one system is inherently regenerative and the other inherently destructive. The peer-reviewed literature is more complicated.

Kgosikoma, Mojeremane and Harvie (2015) compared soil and vegetation characteristics across ranches and adjacent communal grazing land in Botswana. Their results did not show a consistent effect of grazing-management category on several soil properties. Rainfall-related variables, soil carbon and clay content were associated with vegetation and rangeland condition, while degradation indicators occurred in both management contexts.

Kgosikoma, Mojeremane and Harvie (2012) also reported that poor rangeland conditions and bush encroachment were not confined to communal land. Dougill et al. (2016), examining the southern Kalahari, documented bush encroachment, shifts in grass composition and broader landscape changes associated with land use, including the expansion of cattleposts and fenced ranches. Their findings support integrated landscape planning rather than an assumption that a change in tenure or fencing automatically prevents degradation.

International synthesis studies also caution against simplistic claims. Briske et al. (2008) reviewed experimental comparisons of rotational and continuous grazing and argued that rotational grazing was not universally superior across the rangelands examined. Teague et al. (2013), meanwhile, discussed the gap between experimental findings and rancher experience with adaptive multi-paddock management, emphasising scale, management design and the limitations of some research approaches. A 2025 southern African review of 17 studies likewise found contradictory and inconclusive results when continuous and rotational systems were compared, partly because stocking density, herbivore composition, spatial scale and experimental design differed.

The conclusion for Botswana is not that grazing management is irrelevant. It is that a management system should be assessed through its design and outcomes. Relevant questions include whether plants receive adequate recovery under local rainfall conditions, whether grazing pressure is distributed appropriately, whether water points create concentration effects, whether labour and fencing costs are justified, and whether animal performance and vegetation condition improve together.

The phrase "rotational grazing" is not an outcome measure. Neither is "regenerative". The outcome measures are forage recovery, vegetation composition, animal performance, soil and water condition, labour requirements and financial results over time.

### 5. Carrying capacity must be dynamic and site-specific

A fixed stocking rate can be a useful planning reference, but it should never be treated as a permanent property of a ranch. Rainfall, drought history, soil, vegetation, water access, wildlife pressure and previous grazing all influence the amount of forage available. A farm that supported a given herd during a favourable period may not support it after consecutive poor seasons.

Frank and Sarthi (2025) examined rainfall variability, drought, NDVI and livestock population in Botswana's Central District over 1990–2020. Their analysis found seasonal and annual relationships among rainfall metrics, drought indicators, vegetation and livestock populations. The authors reported significant declines in NDVI and cattle and goat numbers in the period studied, while rainfall metrics did not show a significant trend in their analysis. Cattle numbers were negatively correlated with consecutive dry days.

These results show why drought monitoring should combine rainfall, vegetation and livestock observations. They do not provide a universal stocking-rate formula. NDVI is a screening indicator of vegetation greenness and productivity, not a direct measure of forage quality, edible biomass or carrying capacity. It should be ground-truthed, particularly in sparse woody vegetation.

A responsible ranch plan should establish decision triggers before a drought becomes an emergency. Triggers may include declining forage availability, falling body condition, water-supply deterioration, poor reproductive performance and continued rainfall failure. The corresponding actions may include more frequent monitoring, protecting priority breeding stock, early sale of surplus animals, feed budgeting or seeking professional range advice. Numerical thresholds must be locally calibrated rather than invented.

### 6. Preventable livestock losses can erase production gains

Livestock losses are often discussed as a veterinary issue, but they are also a business-management issue. Predation, disease, theft, straying, unknown mortality and poor husbandry can reduce the number of animals available for sale or breeding. They can also represent lost future calves, wasted feed and lost genetic progress.

Mudongo et al. (2025) reported a four-year case study of the Herding for Health model in Eretsha, Botswana. Overall annual cattle losses were reported at 11% in the managed system compared with 32% in free-roaming systems. The study also reported differences in predation and disease losses, net herd size, revenue and rangeland measurements. The authors noted limitations and trade-offs, including reduced nighttime grazing and drought-related stress.

This is valuable local evidence, but it is a specific intervention in a specific setting. It should not be advertised as proof that every farmer will reduce losses by the same proportion. It does, however, justify measuring the causes and financial consequences of losses rather than recording only total herd numbers.

A basic livestock-loss register should record date, animal class, number affected, suspected cause, confirmed veterinary findings where available, location, action taken and evidence source. The distinction between suspected and confirmed cause is essential. The system should not convert a farmer's observation into a clinical diagnosis.

### 7. Climate resilience and financial planning must be integrated

Drought planning is often reduced to buying feed when pasture becomes scarce. That may be necessary in some circumstances, but it is not a complete business strategy. Supplementary feeding has costs: feed purchase, transport, storage, wastage, labour and water. It should be compared with alternative actions, including early destocking, reducing non-productive animals, protecting priority breeding stock and adjusting production targets.

A 2025 systematic review by Zenda examined climate adaptation and mitigation strategies across South African livestock systems. It identified measures including planned destocking, selective breeding, water management, shade, financial planning, feed supplementation and diversification. These are relevant regional options, but their effectiveness and affordability must be tested in Botswana's production contexts.

Drought resilience also depends on liquidity. A farmer may own valuable cattle but lack cash to purchase feed, repair a pump or transport animals. A seasonal cash-flow plan should therefore be developed alongside the grazing plan. The key question is whether the enterprise can finance the actions required to protect its productive assets during a poor season.

### 8. Beef-market performance is linked to disease control and policy

The ranch does not operate in isolation from the beef value chain. Market access depends on buyer specifications, animal condition, traceability, veterinary rules, transport, disease status, processing capacity and price formation.

Dizyee, Baker and Rich (2017) developed a system-dynamics model of policy options for Botswana's beef sector. Their analysis linked cattle biology, marketing, trade, rainfall and animal disease. The model suggested that disease-control improvements could benefit chain actors, while market liberalisation could redistribute gains and losses. Combining market reforms with stronger disease control improved overall financial performance in their scenarios.

Kabelo et al. (2023) reviewed Botswana's foot-and-mouth disease status and control measures for 2006–2022. Their review described the circulation of SAT serotypes, wildlife-livestock interfaces, surveillance and vaccination, and challenges related to livestock and buffalo interactions. This reinforces that veterinary zones and official movement directions are not peripheral details. They directly influence market opportunity and farm economics.

Farmers should know the current buyer's specifications before making finishing decisions. They should also verify current official veterinary rules before moving animals or offering them into a particular market. No general online article can replace current Department of Veterinary Services directions.

### 9. The soil microbiome is an emerging frontier, not a ready-made input claim

A particularly important scientific opportunity is the microbial ecology of Botswana's dryland soils. Coetzee et al. (2024) characterised bacterial and fungal communities across 89 sites in the Sandveld, Wet-Sandveld and Hardveld using 16S rRNA and fungal ITS sequencing. They found that soil pH, precipitation, total organic carbon and soil ions were important correlates of microbial community structure and diversity.

This is a significant baseline for future research. Soil microorganisms contribute to nutrient cycling and other ecosystem processes, but the 2024 study does not demonstrate that a particular microbial product increases pasture production or cattle profitability. Functional experiments, plant-microbe studies and field trials are still needed.

A responsible Botswana bio-intelligence programme could investigate microbial communities associated with productive forage species, drought-affected soils, restoration sites and different grazing histories. Such work should include conservation safeguards, research permissions, benefit-sharing and biosafety. The opportunity is scientific discovery and evidence-led innovation, not premature sale of inoculants.

## Discussion

### 10. A Botswana Ranch Performance Framework

The evidence supports a five-pillar management framework.

**Pillar 1: Financial performance.** Record revenue, variable costs, labour, repairs, feed, veterinary services, transport, capital expenditure and cash-flow timing. Gross margin should be distinguished from net profit. Report margin per hectare or breeding female only where the denominator and cost allocation are reliable.

**Pillar 2: Forage and rangeland productivity.** Monitor vegetation composition, ground cover, forage biomass where measured, seasonal quality, bush encroachment, water distribution and recovery. The monitoring method should be repeatable and appropriate to the ecological zone.

**Pillar 3: Herd and reproductive performance.** Reconcile herd numbers and track breeding females exposed, calving, calf survival, weaning, weight, mortality and culling. Breed decisions should be based on whole-life performance and adaptation rather than fashion.

**Pillar 4: People and operating systems.** Define responsibilities, recordkeeping routines, labour productivity, water and infrastructure maintenance, livestock security and emergency procedures. A sophisticated grazing plan is not useful if it cannot be implemented with available labour and capital.

**Pillar 5: Markets and climate resilience.** Understand buyer requirements, market timing, veterinary restrictions, drought triggers, feed options and cash-flow resilience. A market plan should be connected to the production plan.

The framework is deliberately not a universal grazing prescription. It is a way of ensuring that management decisions are assessed across the whole enterprise.

### 11. What should be measured first?

A low-capital pilot should begin with the records that farmers can realistically provide. The first month should establish herd inventory, breeding females, births, sales, deaths, missing animals, feed purchases, operating expenses, water points and grazing areas. The next stage should document body condition, forage observations and the condition of key infrastructure.

The initial report should show what is known, what is missing and which decisions require a qualified professional. It should not produce a fabricated carrying capacity or a precise profit forecast from incomplete information.

A simple mobile-first, offline record system is likely to be more useful than a complex cloud platform at this stage. Core workflows should operate without internet access and allow the farmer to export their own records. The system should retain the source, date, observer and confidence level for each observation. Any later benchmarking must protect farm privacy and compare only sufficiently similar production contexts.

### 12. Commercial opportunities for Botswana and the region

The research suggests several realistic service opportunities. The first is a ranch performance diagnostic that converts existing farm records into a structured report. The second is a herd reproduction and loss register. The third is a seasonal forage and rangeland monitoring service delivered with competent field partners. The fourth is drought-readiness planning, including cash-flow and feed-cost scenarios. The fifth is buyer-led fodder aggregation, but only after supplier quantity, quality, delivery cost and buyer demand have been verified.

A longer-term opportunity lies in soil microbial research and locally relevant forage innovation. This should be developed through partnerships with universities and qualified laboratories rather than through unsupported product claims.

The strongest initial commercial proposition is likely to be a human-reviewed ranch performance assessment. It requires little capital, can be delivered with simple tools and creates a clear learning loop between the farmer's records and the field realities. Demand, willingness to pay and delivery costs still need validation. No profit increase should be promised before a baseline and follow-up measurement exist.
