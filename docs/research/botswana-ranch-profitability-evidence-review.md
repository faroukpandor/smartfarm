# Botswana Ranch Profitability: Evidence Review and Research Agenda

**Status:** Research synthesis — working draft, not a journal submission  
**Prepared:** 21 November 2026  
**Scope:** Botswana first; transferable Southern African dryland evidence clearly distinguished  
**Purpose:** Ground ranch-profitability content, field assessments and SmartFarm requirements in traceable evidence.

## Executive synthesis

The strongest defensible proposition is not that a particular grazing system guarantees higher profit. It is that ranch performance is a coupled biological, ecological and commercial problem. Botswana-specific studies show that rainfall, forage phenology, soil and vegetation condition, livestock losses, animal genetics, disease status and beef-market structure all influence production and returns. They also warn against simplistic explanations.

Three conclusions should guide all subsequent content and product work:

1. **Measure output and margin, not herd size alone.** Track kilograms of calf weaned per exposed breeding female, mortality, saleable output, gross margin by enterprise and cost per kilogram produced. Where data allow, also report gross margin per hectare and per breeding female. Never compare farms without disclosing land type, rainfall, herd class, tenure, prices and cost-accounting method.
2. **Do not equate paddock subdivision or rotational grazing with restoration.** Botswana research found degradation indicators in both ranch and communal systems and did not find grazing-management category alone consistently explained soil and vegetation properties. Other Botswana studies document bush encroachment, changes in grass composition and land-use effects. Management must be evaluated by outcomes, context and time.
3. **Use adaptive, locally measured decisions.** Rainfall and forage quality vary markedly by season and ecological zone. Drought plans, destocking triggers, water planning, feed decisions and breeding strategies should be tied to field observations and farm economics, not imported stocking-rate tables.

The commercially useful gap is a low-cost, evidence-aware farm assessment and record system that converts a farmer's existing records and field observations into transparent management questions. It should not pretend to diagnose rangeland condition from a single satellite index or prescribe a universal stocking rate.

## 1. Evidence grading and interpretation

This review prioritises peer-reviewed research directly concerning Botswana. Southern African studies are used as contextual evidence only. Extension publications, government statistics, international programmes and the Ranching for Profit/Jim Gerrish material are useful for practice and context, but they are not interchangeable with peer-reviewed causal evidence.

Evidence grades used here:

- **A — Direct Botswana peer-reviewed evidence:** empirical study or review with explicit Botswana scope.
- **B — Regional peer-reviewed evidence:** relevant Southern African research; transferability requires local testing.
- **C — Technical or institutional guidance:** useful for operational practice, but not necessarily peer-reviewed.
- **D — Practitioner framework or hypothesis:** useful to generate questions; claims require independent validation.

The literature is heterogeneous in age, spatial scale, study design and production system. Older Botswana production trials remain valuable baselines but cannot be treated as current national averages. Cross-sectional comparisons do not establish causation. A result from Eretsha cannot be assumed to represent the Kalahari Sandveld, eastern Hardveld or every communal grazing arrangement.

## 2. Botswana evidence synthesis

### 2.1 Ranch versus cattle-post production and the danger of simplistic comparisons

Rennie et al. (1977) compared indigenous-type beef cattle under traditional unenclosed communal grazing (cattle-post) and fenced ranch systems over four years. The reported calving percentages were 46.4% and 74.0%, respectively; seven-month calf weights were 122.5 kg and 177.4 kg; calf mortality was 10.2% and 8.5%. The authors estimated 51 versus 120 kg weaner calf per cow per year and 86 versus 188 kg of 18-month-old calf per cow per year.

**Interpretation:** This is an important historical indication that management and production conditions can materially affect output. It is not proof that fencing itself causes superior performance, nor a current benchmark. Differences may reflect management, selection, veterinary access, grazing resources, labour and other factors. Modern work should replicate the comparison with transparent controls and contemporary economics.

### 2.2 Tswana and crossbred cow performance

Light, Buck and Lethola (2010) analysed seven years of records (1974–1980) for pure Tswana cows and several crossbred groups. Reported calving rates ranged from 0.82 for pure Tswana to 0.90 for Simmental-cross cows; calf survival rates ranged from 0.93 to 0.96. The study assessed mothering ability and productivity as well as reproductive outcomes.

**Interpretation:** These data show why breed comparisons must include more than mature size or sale weight. Calving, calf survival, maternal performance, feed demand, longevity and reproductive regularity matter. The observations are historical and cannot establish the best contemporary genotype under every Botswana environment. A modern breeding decision should compare lifetime kilograms weaned per cow exposed, cow maintenance costs, adaptation, fertility, health and market fit.

### 2.3 Rangeland condition, land tenure and vegetation change

Kgosikoma, Mojeremane and Harvie (2015) sampled soil and vegetation in three ranches and adjacent communal grazing areas in Botswana. Grazing-management categories did not consistently explain soil texture, organic carbon, pH or bulk density. Rainfall-linked factors, including soil carbon and clay content, were associated with herbaceous biomass and bush encroachment. Indicators of degradation occurred in both ranch and communal settings.

Kgosikoma, Mojeremane and Harvie (2012), in a Botswana Journal of Agriculture and Applied Sciences study, similarly reported degraded conditions in several sampled rangelands, including ranch and communal locations; bush encroachment was observed at Matlolakgang and Xanagas. The study cautioned against assuming degradation is confined to communal tenure.

Dougill et al. (2016) used farm-scale transects and landscape-scale analysis in the southern Kalahari. They reported bush encroachment, changes in herbaceous composition, invasive Prosopis glandulosa in parts of the arid southwest, and landscape consequences associated with expansion of cattleposts and fenced ranches. Their conclusion emphasised integrated landscape-scale land-use planning.

**Interpretation:** These studies challenge the simplistic claim that private ranching automatically prevents degradation or that communal tenure alone explains it. Ranch design, water-point distribution, grazing concentration, vegetation type, rainfall history, wildlife interactions, fire, woody plant dynamics and landscape connectivity all matter. Content should distinguish farm-level productivity from wider landscape ecological outcomes.

### 2.4 Forage quality is seasonal and species-specific

A three-year Kalahari study, *Nutrient content and digestibility of forage plants in relation to plant phenology and rainfall in the Kalahari, Botswana* (Journal of Arid Environments, 1986; DOI: 10.1016/S0140-1963(18)31226-6), assessed eight dominant grasses. It found substantial differences among species and seasonal variation associated with phenology and rainfall. The measured nutrient levels were generally low relative to cattle requirements, with calcium an exception; woody species sampled often had higher nutrient content than grasses.

**Interpretation:** Standing biomass is not the same as usable nutrition. A forage assessment should distinguish species composition, growth stage, dry matter availability, crude protein, digestibility and minerals. The old study is a strong reason to commission current local forage sampling, not a basis for prescribing current supplementation rates.

### 2.5 Rainfall, drought, vegetation and livestock

Frank and Sarthi (2025) examined rainfall metrics, drought, NDVI and livestock populations in Botswana's Central District from 1990 to 2020. They found seasonal and annual relationships among rainfall metrics, drought indicators, NDVI and livestock numbers. NDVI and cattle and goat numbers declined significantly in the study period; cattle numbers were negatively correlated with consecutive dry days. The authors reported no significant trend in the rainfall metrics analysed.

**Interpretation:** Drought intelligence should combine rainfall, vegetation and livestock observations. A vegetation index is not a direct measure of forage quality or carrying capacity. Ground-truthing is essential, particularly in sparse vegetation and mixed woody-grass systems. Correlation does not establish that rainfall alone caused herd changes.

### 2.6 Communal pastoralism, livestock losses and market incentives

Mudongo et al. (2025) evaluated a four-year Herding for Health implementation in Eretsha, Okavango Delta. The case study reported overall annual cattle losses of 11% in the Herding for Health system versus 32% in free-roaming systems, with differences in predation, disease and other husbandry-related losses. Free-roaming systems recorded more calves (214 versus 161), while the managed group retained a slightly larger net herd (739 versus 727) and recorded higher revenue (US$30,780 versus US$4,574.64). Planned-grazing areas showed increased herbaceous biomass and ecological value, while the authors also noted limitations including reduced night grazing and drought-related stress.

**Interpretation:** This is a compelling Botswana case, but it is a specific intervention and context, not a universal causal estimate for every community. The lesson is to measure net herd retention, mortality causes, calf output, revenue, labour, grazing condition and market access together. It also demonstrates that management interventions can have trade-offs.

### 2.7 Beef value chains, disease and market access

Dizyee, Baker and Rich (2017) developed a system-dynamics model of Botswana's beef value chain, linking cattle biology, meat marketing, trade, rainfall and animal disease. Their scenarios indicated that disease control benefited chain actors, while market liberalisation could redistribute gains and losses across producers, Botswana Meat Commission and contracted feedlots. Combining market reform with stronger disease control improved whole-chain financial performance in their model.

Kabelo et al. (2023) reviewed Botswana's foot-and-mouth disease status and control measures for 2006–2022. They discussed SAT serotypes, wildlife-livestock interfaces, surveillance, vaccination and challenges in separating domestic livestock from buffalo populations in some areas.

**Interpretation:** Farm profitability cannot be isolated from veterinary zoning, movement controls, disease surveillance, export eligibility and buyer requirements. Content must not encourage livestock movement or trade outside current official veterinary directions. Any market-pricing content needs date-stamped, source-verified data.

### 2.8 Soil microbial diversity: a distinct research opportunity

Coetzee et al. (2024) used bacterial 16S rRNA and fungal ITS amplicon sequencing across 89 sites and three Botswana ecological zones. They identified strong differences in microbial community structure across zones, with soil pH, precipitation, total organic carbon, calcium and magnesium among important correlates. The study provides a landscape-scale baseline for Botswana dryland soil bacterial and fungal communities.

**Interpretation:** This is a significant research foundation, not evidence that a particular microbial inoculant improves pasture or cattle performance. Functional roles, plant-microbe interactions, causal mechanisms and field-level productivity effects remain research questions. Botswana's microbial diversity should be treated as a long-term scientific opportunity, with conservation, benefit-sharing, biosafety and research permissions respected.

## 3. Southern African comparative evidence

A 2025 review by Zenda synthesised 55 studies on livestock climate adaptation and mitigation in South African production systems. It identifies practices including planned destocking, selective breeding, water management, shade, financial planning, feed supplementation and wildlife ranching. It is useful as a regional menu of adaptation options, but not a Botswana-specific effectiveness ranking.

A 2025 review of 17 southern African studies comparing continuous and rotational grazing found contradictory and inconclusive outcomes, in part because stocking density, herbivore composition, spatial scale and experimental comparability differed. This is a crucial warning against marketing rotational grazing as a guaranteed solution.

A 2026 southern African article, *Grazing smarter: science-based grazing strategies, not only stocking rate*, argues for grazing strategies designed around forage quality, recovery, rest and flexible movement. It is a relevant practitioner/scientific debate, but its recommendations must be tested against Botswana's lower-rainfall Sandveld, woody savannas, fire regimes, labour and water constraints before being generalised.

## 4. What this means for a Botswana ranch assessment

A practical assessment should collect, at minimum:

### Enterprise and land
- Land tenure and usable area; distinguish total area from grazeable area.
- Ecological zone, soil/vegetation type, rainfall history and water-point locations.
- Grazing units, access arrangements, wildlife interface and applicable veterinary zone.
- Major infrastructure, condition, capital replacement needs and annual maintenance costs.

### Herd and production
- Opening and closing herd by class and sex.
- Breeding females exposed, bulls, births, weaning, purchases, sales, deaths and missing animals.
- Calving rate with a clearly defined denominator.
- Calf survival to weaning, weaning weight where measured, and mortality causes.
- Body-condition observations, age structure, culling and replacement.
- Feed and mineral purchases and use; record veterinary actions without generating automated treatment advice.

### Economics
- Cash and non-cash costs, labour, transport, water, fuel, repairs, feed, veterinary services and marketing.
- Sales by class, weight, grade and channel; date-stamped prices and deductions.
- Gross margin by enterprise and per hectare where area data are reliable.
- Cost per kilogram of liveweight or carcass output only when weights and cost allocation are defensible.
- Working capital, debt service and cash-flow timing separately from gross margin.

### Rangeland and climate
- Repeatable fixed-point photographs and field transects.
- Species composition and functional group observations.
- Ground cover, bare ground, litter, erosion indicators and bush encroachment.
- Forage biomass using an appropriate locally validated method.
- Forage laboratory analysis when nutrition decisions depend on it.
- Rainfall records and drought indices; satellite indicators treated as screening signals, not ground truth.

## 5. Commercial and product opportunities

1. **Ranch performance diagnostic:** a paid, human-reviewed report using existing records and a field checklist. First validate demand and delivery costs; do not promise a profit uplift.
2. **Herd loss and reproduction register:** simple offline records for calving, weaning, mortality, missing animals and breeding-herd performance.
3. **Forage and veld monitoring service:** repeatable field observations, fixed-point photos, forage sampling referrals and seasonal reports.
4. **Drought readiness planning:** scenario budgets, trigger registers, planned destocking decisions and feed procurement comparisons.
5. **Fodder sourcing and aggregation:** only after supplier quantity, quality, delivery cost, buyer demand and regulatory requirements are verified.
6. **Market and disease-risk intelligence:** date-stamped market requirements, veterinary-zone awareness and official-source links; never substitute for DVS instructions.
7. **Soil microbiome research collaboration:** a longer-horizon research partnership, not a near-term product claim.

## 6. Research gaps requiring further work

- Current, representative farm-level production and cost data by ecological zone and production system.
- Contemporary reproductive performance and lifetime productivity of Tswana and crossbred cattle.
- Locally calibrated forage biomass and quality monitoring protocols.
- Longitudinal comparison of grazing systems that controls for stocking pressure, soil, rainfall, herd type, water and management.
- Economics of planned destocking versus emergency feeding in Botswana.
- Current market-channel margins and buyer specifications, with veterinary-zone constraints included.
- Functional metagenomics and plant-associated microbiomes linked to forage performance under field conditions.
- Adoption costs, labour burden and distributional effects of grazing interventions in communal settings.

## 7. Research and publication integrity rules

- Cite primary sources, not search snippets.
- Verify authors, year, journal, volume, pages and DOI before final academic submission.
- Distinguish peer-reviewed findings from government statistics, technical guidance and practitioner opinion.
- Never present historical sample values as current national averages.
- Never infer causality from correlations or observational case studies.
- Do not invent a carrying capacity, stocking rate, forage budget, market price or profit improvement.
- Clearly disclose illustrative assumptions in all calculators and examples.
- Do not publish identifiable farm or supplier records without consent.
- Veterinary disease, treatment, movement and trade guidance must defer to current official Botswana authorities and qualified professionals.
